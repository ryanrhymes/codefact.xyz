In a fascinating convergence of mathematics and art, mathematicians Inna Zakharevich and Thomas Hull have unfolded a new realm of possibilities with origami – the traditional art of paper folding. Their [groundbreaking research](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.07932v1) has established origami as Turing complete, placing it in the league of universal computation. This means, at least theoretically, origami can perform any calculation a computer can, given enough paper and time.

![Kristina Armitage/Quanta Magazine](https://d2r55xnwy6nx47.cloudfront.net/uploads/2024/01/OrigamiMath-byKristinaArmitage-Lede-scaled.webp "Kristina Armitage/Quanta Magazine")

This revelation is intriguing because it adds a new dimension to our understanding of computation. Turing completeness, a concept named after the British mathematician Alan Turing, implies that a system can simulate a Turing machine - a fundamental model in computer science capable of solving any problem given enough resources. Zakharevich and Hull achieved this by translating computational inputs, outputs, and operations like AND and OR into specific folds in paper. Although the idea of using origami for actual computing tasks like weather prediction or complex mathematical calculations is highly impractical due to its massive inefficiency, the theoretical implications are profound.

But where does this leave us in practical terms? The real power of this discovery lies not in folding massive sheets of paper to solve equations, but in the inspiration it provides for fields like engineering and computer science. The principles of origami, now known to be capable of complex computations, could drive innovative designs in robotics, space technology, and nanotechnology. Imagine robots or space structures that unfold themselves from compact forms or medical devices that navigate through the body by changing shapes.

Moreover, this exploration into origami's computational abilities exemplifies the rich, often unexpected intersections between different fields – in this case, art and science. It reminds us that sometimes, the most groundbreaking innovations come from the most unlikely sources. As we continue to explore these interdisciplinary paths, who knows what other surprising connections we might uncover? In origami's intricate folds and creases, we find a metaphor for the endless possibilities that lie in the amalgamation of different disciplines, waiting to be discovered and explored.
